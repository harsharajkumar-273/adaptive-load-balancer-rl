\begin{abstract}
Production request routers are horizontally scaled: many router replicas
decide independently from the same, slightly stale view of backend state, and
they increasingly score backends with learned models. Classic queueing results
show that shortest-queue routing on stale shared information \emph{herds}:
every router picks the same server. We ask whether learned routers inherit this
failure. They do, and it is worse. With 16 routers and 50\,ms-stale state,
argmin routing on learned latency predictions has a P99 of 1.1\,s against
0.8\,s for join-the-shortest-queue, and 12.5\,s against 3.1\,s at 200\,ms.
We show why: any policy that is a deterministic function of the shared snapshot
sends a whole refresh window to one server, whereas restricting each request to
candidates drawn independently of the snapshot caps a server's burst at
$\kappa\lambda\Delta$; measured burst shares agree with both statements.
Richer latency models and optimistic exploration do not fix argmin herding.
Two remedies do: learned power-of-$d$ routing, and an age-aware stochastic split
that accounts for the arrivals a snapshot cannot yet reflect. Without any
probing, learned power-of-$d$ keeps steady-state P99 between 108 and 282\,ms across
28 combinations of staleness, router count and refresh phase. In LLM serving
with prefix caches, a locality-preserving variant stays within 2$\times$ of
the best tuned prefix-affinity threshold in all of 108 workload regimes with no
tuning, while every fixed threshold is at least 2.5$\times$ worse in some regime.
With three live probes it matches an oracle that knows every server's speed
(84 vs.\ 83\,ms) and beats a Prequal-style prober at equal probe cost under a
gray failure (112 vs.\ 144\,ms). The study covers 46{,}930 simulated runs and a
multi-process prototype.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Every request to a large online service passes through a routing tier that
decides which backend replica serves it. That tier is itself a distributed
system: tens to hundreds of gateway or sidecar replicas sit behind an L4 load
balancer, and each decides on its own. Because backends cannot be polled on
every request, gateways share a periodically refreshed view of backend load
(e.g.\ heartbeats written to a key-value store) and route on that.

Two trends meet in this layer. First, routing decisions are increasingly
\emph{learned}: bandit-based and model-based routers predict per-backend latency
from context and route to the predicted best backend, and LLM-serving routers
score replicas by predicted cache reuse and load~\citep{llmd,vllmrouter,lodestar2026}.
Second, these routers are deployed as \emph{many independent replicas}, each
with its own model trained on the traffic it happened to see.

Queueing theory has long warned that combining the two ingredients of this
setting, stale shared information and many dispatchers, is dangerous.
Join-the-shortest-queue (JSQ) on old information causes \emph{herding}: all
dispatchers agree on the currently shortest queue and flood it until the next
update~\citep{mitzenmacher2000old,dahlin2000stale}. Randomised power-of-two
choices is the classic antidote~\citep{mitzenmacher2001power}. It is not known
whether learned routers, which add a second, slower feedback loop (the model),
behave like JSQ, better, or worse.

\paragraph{This paper.} We study herding in horizontally scaled learned
routers, in a queueing model, in an LLM-serving model with prefix caches, and
on a multi-process prototype. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Characterisation.} Argmin routing on learned latency predictions
  herds \emph{worse} than classic JSQ under stale shared state
  (\cref{sec:eval-herding}). It is not fixed by desynchronising refresh phases
  once there are many routers, and a control-plane interval, as learned
  routers commonly use, adds staleness of its own (\cref{sec:eval-epoch}).
\item \textbf{A burst bound.} Any policy that is a deterministic function of the
  shared snapshot sends an entire refresh window to one server; any policy that
  restricts each request to candidates drawn independently of the snapshot caps
  a server's burst at $\kappa_s\lambda\Delta$ (\cref{sec:theory}). Measured
  burst shares agree with both statements (\cref{sec:eval-burst}).
\item \textbf{The learner is not the problem.} Richer latency models and
  optimistic exploration do not fix argmin herding, and some make it much worse;
  learned power-of-$d$ is insensitive to the model class (\cref{sec:eval-models}).
\item \textbf{Remedies.} \emph{Learned power-of-$d$} (sample $d$ candidates,
  rank them with the learned model) and an \emph{age-aware stochastic split}
  that accounts for the arrivals the snapshot cannot yet reflect
  (\cref{sec:design}). For LLM serving we add \emph{ring-$d$}, which draws the
  candidates from a prefix's rendezvous-hash replicas to keep cache locality.
\item \textbf{Evaluation.} Across the three settings the remedies are the most
  robust routers we tested. In LLM serving, learned ring-2 stays within 2$\times$
  of the best tuned prefix-affinity threshold in all 108 workload regimes with no
  tuning, while every fixed threshold is at least 2.5$\times$ worse (up to
  133$\times$) in some regime (\cref{sec:eval-llm}). Our probing variant beats a Prequal-style
  prober~\citep{prequal2024} at equal probe cost (\cref{sec:eval-probing}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Setting}
\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Routing tier.} $K$ gateways receive requests from an L4 balancer,
which spreads them roughly uniformly. Each gateway picks one of $N$ backends
with heterogeneous service rates $\mu_1,\dots,\mu_N$.

\paragraph{Information.} Gateways share a \emph{snapshot} of backend queue
lengths that is refreshed every $\Delta$ seconds. Staleness comes from
heartbeat and polling periods and from control-plane intervals that recompute
routing weights. Refreshes may be \emph{synchronised} (every gateway sees the
same snapshot at the same time, e.g.\ a control plane pushing weights) or
\emph{unsynchronised} (each gateway polls on its own phase). Each gateway also
observes the latency of the requests it proxied, when they complete.

\paragraph{Learned routing.} A learned gateway keeps, per backend $s$, a model
predicting latency from context. We use the simplest model that captures
heterogeneity: a discounted Bayesian linear regression
$\ell/\bar\ell \sim \theta_s^\top [1, q_s]$, where $q_s$ is the snapshot queue
length the gateway saw when it routed and $\bar\ell$ is a latency unit. The
slope learns $1/\mu_s$; the discount tracks drift. On top of the model, a
gateway can route greedily (argmin of the posterior mean), by per-request
Thompson sampling (TS), or, as in many deployed learned routers, by
recomputing softmax weights from a posterior sample every control interval
and routing by those weights in between (\emph{TS-epoch}).

\paragraph{Probing.} Prequal~\citep{prequal2024} avoids shared state entirely:
each query triggers asynchronous probes that return a backend's
requests-in-flight (RIF) and its latency estimate; responses are pooled and
selected by a hot--cold lexicographic rule. Probing trades message cost for
freshness.

\section{A Burst Bound for Routing on Shared State}
\label{sec:theory}

Requests arrive as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda$ and are spread uniformly
over $K$ routers. All routers read the same snapshot, refreshed every $\Delta$.
In one window between refreshes let $A\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda\Delta)$ be the
arrivals and $B_s$ the number sent to server $s$.

\begin{proposition}[Common information implies a stampede]\label{prop:stampede}
If every router's choice is the same deterministic function of the common
snapshot (JSQ, SED, and learned argmin routers whose models agree), then in every
window all $A$ arrivals go to one server: $\max_s B_s=A$ and $\mathbb{E}[A]=\lambda\Delta$,
for any number of routers $K$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The same function applied to the same input returns the same server for every
request in the window.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A bounded candidate probability caps the burst]\label{prop:cap}
Suppose each request is routed within a candidate set $S$ drawn independently of
the snapshot and of earlier decisions, and let $\kappa_s=\Pr[s\in S]$. Then
$B_s$ is stochastically dominated by a $\mathrm{Poisson}(\kappa_s\lambda\Delta)$
variable. In particular $\mathbb{E}[B_s]\le\kappa_s\lambda\Delta$, and with
$\mu=\kappa_s\lambda\Delta$, $\Pr[B_s\ge m]\le e^{-\mu}(e\mu/m)^m$ for $m>\mu$.
For $d$ candidates drawn uniformly from $N$ servers, $\kappa_s=d/N$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Each arrival is a candidate for $s$ independently with probability $\kappa_s$ and
can be routed to $s$ only then. $B_s$ is at most the number of such arrivals,
which is a Poisson thinning of the arrival process.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Consequences.} (i) The cap holds for any number of routers, any
staleness, any refresh phases, and any learned model: the candidate draw does
not read the snapshot. (ii) A burst of $B$ requests delays the last of them at a
server of rate $\mu_s$ by at least $(B-\mu_s\Delta)^+/\mu_s$. With the
parameters of our queueing study ($\lambda=900$\,req/s, $\Delta=200$\,ms) a
deterministic router puts at least $(900-200)\cdot0.2/200=0.7$\,s on the fastest
server, whereas $d=2$ of $N=8$ caps its burst at 45 requests, about what that
server drains in a window. (iii) The cap bounds the damage of a \emph{wrong}
ranking; it does not stop a slow server from receiving up to $\kappa_s$ of the
traffic. Keeping slow servers out is the learned score's job.
(iv) Learned argmin routers are not covered by \cref{prop:stampede} when their
models differ, because the choice then depends on private state. How much that
decorrelates the routers is an empirical question (\cref{sec:eval-burst}).
(v) With request-dependent candidates, $\kappa_s$ is the probability that a
request's candidate set contains $s$; for LLM serving it is the popularity mass of
the prefixes whose candidate replicas include $s$ (\cref{sec:design}).

\paragraph{Two mechanisms.} \emph{Within a router}, the inputs do not change
between refreshes, so a deterministic argmin sends every request that router
receives in the window, about $\lambda\Delta/K$ of them, to the same server.
Counting its own sends since the last refresh (\emph{local correction})
removes this. \emph{Across routers}, the shared snapshot makes all $K$ argmins
agree (\cref{prop:stampede}), and local correction cannot help because a router
does not see the others' sends. Learning can make this worse. The model
regresses latency on the \emph{snapshot} queue length, so a burst sent to a
server that looked short is attributed to a short queue; the model's own
feedback loop is slower than the snapshot and amplifies the oscillation.

\section{Remedies}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Learned power-of-$d$.} Sample $d$ backends uniformly, score each with
the posterior-mean predicted latency, route to the lowest. The snapshot variant
scores with the shared (stale) queue lengths, the probing variant reads the $d$
candidates' live queue lengths ($d$ probes per request). By \cref{prop:cap}
any backend is a candidate with probability $d/N$.

\paragraph{Age-aware stochastic split.} Instead of sampling candidates, each
router can randomise \emph{how much} to send to each server. A request arriving
$a$ seconds after the snapshot sees, in a fluid approximation where every router
uses the same split $p$, queue $Q_i=\max(0,q_i+(\lambda p_i-\mu_i)a)$ at server $i$,
hence expected delay $(Q_i+1)/\mu_i$. Equalising the delay at level $L$ gives
\[
p_i=\max\Bigl(0,\frac{\mu_i(L+a)-q_i-1}{\lambda a}\Bigr),\qquad \textstyle\sum_i p_i=1,
\]
which we solve exactly by sorting breakpoints. As $a\to0$ this tends to
shortest-expected-delay, and as $a\to\infty$ to a capacity-proportional random
split. Each router estimates $\lambda$ as $K$ times its own smoothed arrival
rate. Service rates are the nominal ones or are learned: the slope of the
latency model is the service time per queue slot. This is our own construction,
in the spirit of stochastically coordinated dispatching~\citep{goren2021stochastic}
but not their algorithm. It satisfies no a-priori cap, but its split
approaches the capacity shares as the snapshot ages.

\paragraph{Locality-preserving candidates for LLM serving.} Uniform sampling
ignores the prefix cache, and in our LLM simulator it reaches a hit rate of only
0.80--0.91 against 0.99 for the design below. \emph{Ring-$d$}
draws the candidates from the $d$ replicas with the highest rendezvous hash for
the request's prefix (the same $d$ at every router), then ranks them by a learned
latency model with features $[1,\text{queue},\text{cache hit}]$. A prefix thus
lives on at most $d$ replicas and the router balances load among them.
By \cref{prop:cap}, $\kappa_s$ is the popularity mass of the prefixes whose ring
contains $s$.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup}
\paragraph{Simulator.} We use an exact discrete-event simulation of $N=8$ FIFO
backends with rates 200/200/150/150/100/100/50/50\,req/s, Poisson arrivals at
$\rho=0.9$ of capacity, $K\in\{1,4,16,64\}$ gateways, and a snapshot period
$\Delta\in\{0,10,50,200\}$\,ms with synchronised or unsynchronised refresh.
Each run simulates 30\,s and discards the first 3\,s. Every reported number is
the median over 10 seeds; figures shade the interquartile range. We report
medians because herding produces multi-second outliers that dominate means.
The simulator matches M/M/1 theory, and it reproduces the classic result that
stale JSQ herds while power-of-two does not. In the \emph{gray failure}
scenario, the fastest backend serves at 20\% speed from $t{=}12$\,s. That
scenario runs at $\rho=0.75$ so the system stays stable (89\% utilisation
after the failure). We measure herding with the Fano factor (variance/mean)
of per-backend arrival counts in 50\,ms bins: independent routing gives about
1, and a stampede gives far more. In total the study has 46{,}930 simulated
runs.

\paragraph{Policies.} \emph{Heuristics:} JSQ, SED (shortest expected delay with
\emph{nominal} speeds), and P2C, all on the shared snapshot. \emph{Learned}
(\cref{sec:background}): greedy, per-request TS, TS-epoch (150\,ms interval),
and learned power-of-$d$ (P$d$C-L) on the snapshot. \emph{Probing} (these
ignore the snapshot): a Prequal-style prober, JSQ($d$) and SED($d$) over $d$
live probes, and learned power-of-$d$ over $d$ live probes (P$d$C-L-probe).
The suffix +lc adds local correction. We tuned Prequal in its favour: its
reuse budget of 1 was the best of $\{1,2,4\}$. It uses 3 probes per request
and a RIF quantile of 0.84, and it is insensitive to that quantile in
$[0.75,0.99]$. We likewise tuned TS-epoch's softmax temperature in its favour.
Probing policies are charged one probe round-trip (0.5\,ms) of latency per
request; Prequal is not, because its probes are asynchronous.

\subsection{Learned routers herd}
\label{sec:eval-herding}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig1_p99_vs_staleness.png}
  \caption{P99 latency against snapshot staleness, with 16 gateways and $\rho=0.9$. Left: every gateway
  refreshes at the same instants. Right: each gateway refreshes on its own phase.}
  \label{fig:staleness}
\end{figure*}

\Cref{fig:staleness} (left) shows the synchronised case. With live state,
learned greedy (75\,ms) and TS (72\,ms) match SED, which is told the true
speeds (69\,ms). Once state is stale, argmin routing collapses. At 50\,ms,
JSQ reaches 836\,ms and \emph{learned greedy 1{,}055\,ms}. At 200\,ms they
reach 3.1\,s and 12.5\,s. The herding index confirms these are stampedes: at
50\,ms it is 30.8 for JSQ, 9.4 for greedy and 8.1 for TS, against 2.5 for P2C
and 2.0 for P2C-L. Per-request TS holds up to 50\,ms (201\,ms) but collapses at
200\,ms (2.6\,s).

\paragraph{Desynchronising refresh is not enough.} Unsynchronised refresh
(\cref{fig:staleness}, right) removes most of the classic herding: at 50\,ms
JSQ improves from 836 to 282\,ms. Learned greedy, however, still reaches
6.1\,s at 200\,ms, and 7.0\,s with 64 gateways at 50\,ms. P2C-L stays at
142--193\,ms across all staleness levels.

\subsection{Scaling the number of gateways}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig2_p99_vs_dispatchers.png}
  \caption{P99 latency against the number of gateways at 50\,ms staleness.}
  \label{fig:gateways}
\end{figure*}
\Cref{fig:gateways} separates the two herding mechanisms from
\cref{sec:theory}.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Within a gateway.} With one gateway, learned greedy (630\,ms)
  and TS (763\,ms) are poor, because all of that gateway's requests between
  refreshes go to one backend. Local correction fixes this: greedy+lc reaches
  100\,ms and TS+lc 103\,ms.
\item \textbf{Across gateways.} Local correction cannot see other gateways'
  sends, so greedy+lc degrades to 8.1\,s at $K{=}64$. TS instead
  \emph{improves} from $K{=}1$ to $K{=}16$ (763 to 201\,ms): independently
  trained posteriors disagree, which decorrelates the gateways.
\item \textbf{Learned power-of-$d$.} P2C-L varies only between 151 and
  180\,ms across $K$, as \cref{prop:cap} predicts.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Measured burst concentration}
\label{sec:eval-burst}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig15_burst_share.png}
  \caption{The busiest server's share of a 200\,ms window's arrivals (bars) against the
  cap of \cref{prop:cap} (black ticks), 16 routers, synchronised stale state.}
  \label{fig:burst}
\end{figure}
\Cref{fig:burst} tests \cref{prop:stampede,prop:cap} directly: for each 200\,ms
window we take the busiest server's share of that window's arrivals.
Capacity-weighted random routing gives 0.22 (its cap is the largest capacity share,
0.20) and P2C 0.26 against its cap of $2/8=0.25$. Learned P2C gives 0.25 and
learned P3C 0.32 (cap 0.375): sampling pins the burst to $d/N$, with the small
excess being sampling noise in windows of about 180 requests. The deterministic routers
stampede: JSQ gives 0.79 and SED 0.96, and their 95th-percentile share is 1.00. Learned argmin routers fall in between: greedy 0.43 and
Thompson sampling 0.46, because their models differ across routers and
decorrelate the choices, as remark (iv) anticipated, but not enough to prevent
the P99 collapse of \cref{fig:staleness}. The age-aware split gives 0.29 with
learned rates and 0.33 with nominal rates. For LLM serving (moderate skew, 200\,ms windows) the cap is the
popularity mass $\kappa_{\max}$ of the prefixes whose candidate set includes a
replica: static hashing has $\kappa_{\max}=0.34$ (measured 0.37), ring-2 0.39 (0.28),
ring-3 0.57 (0.29), uniform P2C 0.25 (0.28) and P3C 0.375 (0.30), JSQ 1.0 (0.66).
Measured shares stay at or below the cap, except by a few points where windows
hold only about 19 requests and sampling noise raises the busiest share.

\subsection{Richer models and optimism do not fix argmin herding}
\label{sec:eval-models}
A natural objection is that the learner is too simple. We repeat the
comparison with a quadratic queue term, with the router's own smoothed latency
for each server as a third feature, and with an optimistic (LinUCB-style,
lower-confidence-bound) learner, at 50 and 200\,ms staleness with 16
routers. \Cref{tab:models} summarises the steady-state results
(\cref{fig:models} in the appendix has the full set).

\centering
\small

\label{tab:models}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule

A richer model never rescues argmin routing, and the quadratic one makes it
far worse, because its fit oscillates with the herding it causes. Optimism
herds too (13.6\,s at 50\,ms). Power-of-$d$ ranges over 141--153\,ms at 50\,ms
and 225--279\,ms at 200\,ms regardless of the model. This matches
\cref{prop:stampede}: herding is a property of acting deterministically on
common information, not of the learner.

\subsection{Control intervals are hidden staleness}
\label{sec:eval-epoch}
TS-epoch recomputes weights every control interval and routes by those weights
in between, as learned-router control planes commonly do. Even with
\emph{live} backend state, its P99 is 137, 149, 204 and 800\,ms for intervals of
10, 50, 150 and 500\,ms, against 72\,ms for per-request TS. The interval acts
as a second staleness knob, on top of the state refresh period: with 50\,ms
state staleness the same intervals give 156 to 1{,}107\,ms. TS-epoch also
herds at scale (348\,ms at 64 gateways, with an IQR reaching 5.8\,s).

\subsection{Gray failure}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig5_gray_failure.png}
  \caption{Gray failure: the fastest backend drops to 20\% speed at $t{=}12$\,s ($\rho=0.75$, 16 gateways).}
  \label{fig:gray}
\end{figure*}
When a backend slows down without announcement (\cref{fig:gray}), policies that
trust nominal speeds keep feeding it. SED rises from 69\,ms in steady state to
146\,ms with live state. Learned models notice the slowdown from latency
alone: greedy reaches 89\,ms and TS 91\,ms. Once state is stale, the herding
results repeat. With synchronised refresh at 200\,ms, greedy reaches 3.2\,s
and TS 4.8\,s, while P2C-L stays at 411\,ms.

\subsection{Probe cost, and learning versus probing}
\label{sec:eval-probing}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig7_probe_frontier.png}
  \caption{P99 latency against probes per request (16 gateways). Snapshot-based policies (at $x{=}0$) use
  50\,ms-stale shared state.}
  \label{fig:frontier}
\end{figure*}
\Cref{fig:frontier} compares policies at equal probe cost. It also separates
what learning contributes from what probing contributes.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Zero probes.} P2C-L (151\,ms steady, 221\,ms gray) beats
  Prequal limited to one probe per request (180 and 230\,ms). Prequal
  degrades sharply below one probe per request (1.0\,s at 0.5).
\item \textbf{Equal budgets.} At 3 probes per request, P3C-L-probe gets
  84\,ms against Prequal's 127\,ms in steady state, and 112 against 144\,ms
  under gray failure.
\item \textbf{Learning versus probing.} In steady state, P3C-L-probe matches
  SED(3), which is \emph{told} the true speeds (84 vs.\ 83\,ms), and beats
  JSQ(3), which ignores heterogeneity (147\,ms). So the learned model recovers
  the oracle's knowledge. Under gray failure the oracle's knowledge goes stale:
  SED(3) rises to 206\,ms while P3C-L-probe holds at 112\,ms.
\end{itemize}
Learning therefore pays off when server speeds are unknown or drifting, and
the sampling step supplies robustness to staleness.

Probing policies do not scale equally. From 1 to 64 gateways, Prequal's P99
rises from 89 to 186\,ms and P3C-L-probe's from 82 to 99\,ms. P3C-L-probe
degrades with $K$ because each gateway learns from $1/K$ of the traffic.

\subsection{Age-aware stochastic split}
\label{sec:eval-awf}
\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig13_age_aware.png}
  \caption{Age-aware stochastic split against SED, JSQ and learned power-of-$d$ on the shared snapshot (16 routers).}
  \label{fig:awf}
\end{figure*}
\Cref{fig:awf} compares the age-aware split with power-of-$d$ on the same stale
snapshot. With learned service rates it is the best router in every
gray-failure cell: 90/120/163/375\,ms at 0/10/50/200\,ms staleness with
synchronised refresh, against 110/131/196/533\,ms for P3C-L and
167/178/221/411\,ms for P2C-L; with unsynchronised refresh 90/114/147/213\,ms
against 110/127/169/283\,ms for P3C-L. Known nominal rates do not help
there (727\,ms at 200\,ms), because after the failure they are wrong. In steady state
with unsynchronised refresh the split with nominal rates is best (112\,ms at
50\,ms and 147\,ms at 200\,ms, against 142 and 193\,ms for P2C-L), and with live
state it beats P2C-L (72 and 76\,ms against 112\,ms). It loses only in
steady state with synchronised refresh at 200\,ms (296\,ms against 238\,ms for P2C-L), where
the burst cap of \cref{prop:cap} is worth more than its extra information.
So the two remedies are complementary: power-of-$d$ needs no model of arrivals,
the age-aware split uses the snapshot's age and learned rates to track a
changing fleet.

\subsection{LLM serving with prefix caches}
\label{sec:eval-llm}
\paragraph{Setup.} $N=8$ prefill replicas, each a FIFO server with an LRU cache of
24 prefixes. Requests have one of 64 shared prefixes (Zipf popularity;
log-uniform length 500--4000 tokens) plus a unique suffix (mean 300 tokens).
A replica that holds the prefix prefills only the suffix, otherwise the whole
prompt, at 8{,}000 tokens/s plus 10\,ms. TTFT is queueing delay plus prefill
time. Each of 16 routers sees a snapshot of every replica's queue length
\emph{and} cached-prefix set, refreshed every $\Delta$, so cache metadata is as
stale as load. We use two regimes, moderate skew (Zipf 1.1, 75\,req/s) and a hot
prefix (Zipf 1.4, 90\,req/s), each with and without a gray failure (one replica
slows to 25\% at $t{=}20$\,s). The learned routers fit one pooled model with
features $[1,\text{queue},\text{cache hit}]$ per router. Baselines:
cache-oblivious JSQ and P2C; static rendezvous hashing; prefix affinity (least
loaded holder); and affinity with a load threshold $T$, the balance rule used in
production LLM routers~\citep{vllmrouter}.

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig11_llm_staleness.png}
  \caption{P99 time to first token, 16 routers, 8 prefill replicas, synchronised refresh.}
  \label{fig:llm}
\end{figure*}
\paragraph{Herding appears in the familiar places (\cref{fig:llm}).}
Cache-oblivious routing loses locality (hit rate 0.75, P99 2.3\,s under
moderate skew with live state), and static hashing concentrates the hot
prefixes on single replicas (10.5\,s and 38\,s). Plain prefix affinity herds
onto the replica that holds a hot prefix: 1.0\,s (moderate) and 26.9\,s (hot
prefix) with live state, against 237 and 252\,ms for affinity with $T{=}4$.
Staleness \emph{helps} plain affinity (377\,ms and 601\,ms at 200\,ms),
apparently because stale cache metadata makes routers replicate hot prefixes
onto more replicas by accident. Learned greedy is the best router with fresh
state (203\,ms against 237\,ms for $T{=}4$ under moderate skew) but herds as
state ages: 425\,ms at 50\,ms and 2.4\,s at 200\,ms.
\paragraph{Ring-$d$ keeps both locality and balance.} Learned ring-3 stays at
224/278/436\,ms (live, 50\,ms, 200\,ms) under moderate skew, close to
affinity with $T{=}4$ (237/261/346\,ms), and with a gray failure it beats it at
every staleness level (336/365/498\,ms against 479/540/594\,ms), since the
learned model notices the slow replica. With the hot prefix the two are
within 1.4$\times$ (ring-3 246/338/567\,ms; $T{=}4$ 252/285/485\,ms).
Uniform power-of-two over all replicas keeps balance but loses locality (hit rate 0.80--0.91).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig12_llm_regret.png}
  \caption{Cumulative distribution over 108 workload regimes of P99 relative to the best tuned
  threshold in that regime (threshold chosen on held-out seeds).}
  \label{fig:regret}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{No single threshold is safe, and ring-$d$ needs none (\cref{fig:regret}).}
Production routers need a threshold $T$, and the right value depends on the
workload. We sweep 108 regimes: Zipf in $\{0.8,1.1,1.4\}$, 60/75/90\,req/s,
cache capacity in $\{16,24,48\}$, 50 or 200\,ms staleness, with and without a gray
failure. For each regime we pick the best $T\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ on seeds 0--4 and
evaluate every router on seeds 5--9. Five different values of $T$ win in
different regimes ($T{=}4$ in 40, $T{=}2$ in 32, $T{=}8$ in 17, $T{=}16$ in 9, and
$T{=}1$ and $T{=}32$ in 5 each). The best single value, $T{=}4$, has median
regret $1.00\times$ and is within $1.5\times$ in 94\% of regimes, but is
$11.2\times$ worse in the worst regime. Other fixed values are
2.5$\times$ to 133$\times$ worse in their worst regime. Learned
ring-2 has median regret 1.06$\times$, 90th percentile 1.30$\times$, 95\% of
regimes within 1.5$\times$, and a worst case of 1.98$\times$; ring-3 has median
1.04$\times$ and worst case 4.3$\times$. Learned greedy (median 2.9$\times$, worst 167$\times$) and plain
affinity (median 3.5$\times$, worst 75$\times$) do not come close.
Choosing the threshold in hindsight on the evaluation seeds, which favours the
thresholds, gives the same picture.

\subsection{Robustness}
\paragraph{Heavy-tailed service.} We use lognormal service times with
coefficient of variation 2 and 4, at 50\,ms staleness with synchronised
refresh. Every policy's tail grows. The ranking among snapshot policies holds:
P2C-L gets 325 and 772\,ms, against 1{,}055 to 8{,}342\,ms for greedy. Among
probing policies, however, the oracle SED(3) now leads (186 and 419\,ms),
ahead of P3C-L-probe (190 and 483\,ms) and Prequal (262 and 598\,ms). Noisy
service slows learning; this is the cost of not knowing the speeds.

\paragraph{Bursty arrivals.} We add a two-state Markov-modulated arrival
process with $\pm25\%$ rate swings at $\rho=0.75$. The conclusions are
unchanged (\texttt{research/results/summary.md}).

\paragraph{Load.} From $\rho=0.5$ to $0.95$, P2C-L rises from 70 to 191\,ms
and P3C-L-probe from 45 to 124\,ms. Learned greedy becomes unstable from
$\rho=0.9$ (3.9\,s at 0.95).

\subsection{Real-system validation}
\label{sec:eval-real}
\paragraph{Prototype.} We repeat the simulator's grid on a real deployment.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Backends:} 8 backend processes, each serving one request at a
  time in FIFO order with exponential service times.
\item \textbf{Gateways:} $K\in\{1,4,16,64\}$ gateways (FastAPI;
  up to 8 independent gateways per process), sharing one Redis instance
  that backends heartbeat into.
\item \textbf{Load:} an open-loop Poisson load generator.
\item \textbf{Strategies:} every gateway strategy is implemented in the
  gateway itself: snapshot, learned, local correction, and probing,
  including a Prequal-style asynchronous probe pool. LinTS-epoch is the
  production control-plane agent running as a separate process.
\item \textbf{Time scale:} everything runs 10$\times$ slower than the
  simulator (backend rates 20..5\,req/s), so single-threaded Python processes
  have headroom. Refresh periods map from 10/50/200\,ms to 0.1/0.5/2\,s.
  Synchronised refresh aligns gateway polling to wall-clock boundaries.
\item \textbf{Runs:} each run measures 90\,s after a 15\,s warm-up, with 3
  seeds per configuration, at $\rho=0.9$ (steady) and $\rho=0.75$ (gray
  failure).
\item \textbf{Isolation:} two runs execute in parallel, each pinned to its
  own CPU cores, Redis database and ports.
\item \textbf{Censoring:} requests still waiting 120\,s after the load stops
  are recorded as censored at their waiting time, so tails of collapsed
  runs are lower bounds.
\end{itemize}
One implementation detail mattered. With a widely used Python HTTP client
whose connection pool scans every open connection per request, stale-state
bursts made the gateway CPU-bound and inflated latencies about 100$\times$.
We therefore use a client with a constant-time connection pool.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Load balancing with old information.} Mitzenmacher studied
dispatching on queue lengths that are $T$ seconds old and found that a small
amount of information goes a long way: sending each task to the less loaded
of two randomly chosen servers is highly effective across a wide range of
parameters, while acting on the apparently least-loaded server is
not~\citep{mitzenmacher2000old}. Dahlin showed that interpreting load
information according to its age avoids pathological behaviour even when the
information is very old~\citep{dahlin2000stale}. Power-of-$d$ choices gives
an exponential improvement over random assignment~\citep{mitzenmacher2001power}.
We show that learned routers inherit, and amplify, the failure of acting on
the apparently best server. The power-of-$d$ remedy still applies, now as a
filter in front of a learned score.

\paragraph{Multiple dispatchers.} Zhou et al.\ give asymptotically optimal
policies for large heterogeneous systems with many
dispatchers~\citep{zhou2021multidispatcher}. Goren et al.\ observe that
herding arises because dispatchers ignore that others act concurrently, and
propose stochastically coordinated dispatching~\citep{goren2021stochastic}.
Tahir et al.\ model many load balancers acting on delayed information as a
mean-field control problem and learn a dispatching policy with policy
gradients~\citep{tahir2022meanfield}. That work designs one policy for all
dispatchers, trained offline on a model of the system. We instead study the
policies that deployed learned routers actually run, each gateway fitting its
own latency model online. We show how independently trained models create a
second, cross-gateway herding mechanism that coordination-free randomisation
removes.

\paragraph{Learning service rates.} Van Kempen et al.\ learn the SED policy
online for two servers with unknown rates and achieve finite regret by
interleaving forced exploration~\citep{vankempen2026adaptivesed}. Their
setting has one dispatcher with fresh state. Ours has many dispatchers with
stale shared state. The learned-versus-oracle comparison in
\cref{fig:frontier} is the multi-dispatcher counterpart of their question.

\paragraph{Replica selection and probing.} C3 ranks replicas by
server-reported feedback combined with rate control~\citep{c3nsdi2015}.
Prequal probes servers asynchronously for requests-in-flight and estimated
latency, reuses probes, and selects with a hot--cold lexicographic
rule~\citep{prequal2024}. Dodoor schedules from cached server load that is
refreshed in batches, trading freshness for fewer
messages~\citep{da2025dodoor}. We compare against a Prequal re-implementation
at equal probe budgets. Learned power-of-$d$ on stale shared state is another
point on the freshness/message trade-off.

\paragraph{Learned and LLM-serving routers.} Learned Load Balancing trains
models that choose datacenter network paths and adapt to
failures~\citep{chang2024llb}. QEdgeProxy runs one proxy per node, formulates
routing as a multi-player bandit, and routes across a \emph{pool} of instances
likely to meet QoS rather than a single best one~\citep{cilic2025qedgeproxy}.
Routers for LLM inference score replicas by cache reuse and load and are
deployed as horizontally scaled services~\citep{llmd,vllmrouter}. Lodestar
learns LLM request routing online and adapts to workload
changes~\citep{lodestar2026}. Our results apply to all of these whenever many
router replicas act on shared, delayed signals. We suggest pairing learned
scores with random candidate sampling, and publishing weights at short
intervals or not at all.

\section{Limitations}
Our backends are single-server FIFO queues; real backends are multi-core.
Our latency models are linear, and although the quadratic, own-latency and
optimistic variants do not change the conclusions, a deep model trained on
rich request features might. The LLM-serving simulator models prefill
replicas only: there is no batching, no decode phase, no KV-cache memory
pressure beyond a prefix count, and one load signal (queue length). Its
constants (8{,}000 tokens/s, Zipf popularity) are plausible, not measured from a
production trace, and a real trace could change which regimes matter.
The age-aware split needs the number of routers and a good estimate of the
total arrival rate. The Prequal baseline is our re-implementation
from the paper's description and is tuned in its favour, but it is not the
production system. Our probing variants probe synchronously; with sub-millisecond
service times the probe round-trip, which Prequal avoids, would matter more
than in our setting. The prototype runs on a 10$\times$ slower time scale than
the simulator, so that single-threaded Python gateways keep headroom. Each
prototype run covers about a third of a simulator run in scaled time, which
emphasises transients such as learning after a gray failure.

\section{Conclusion}
Horizontally scaled learned routers are exposed to herding in two ways.
Within a gateway, requests pile onto one backend between refreshes. Across
gateways, models trained on similar data reach the same answer. Control
intervals add staleness of their own. The remedy is cheap: keep the learned
score for \emph{ranking}, but let random candidate sampling decide
\emph{which} backends are ranked. This bounds any backend's share of traffic
independently of how stale or how widely shared the state is, and it keeps
the benefit of learning when server speeds are unknown or change.
